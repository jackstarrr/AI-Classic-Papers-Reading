Detecting toxic visual content on social media demands both high accuracy and low latency. While Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) offer fast inference, they struggle with semantically complex images containing context, symbols, or embedded text. Conversely, Vision-Language Models (VLMs) provide strong semantic reasoning but incur 10-100× higher computational costs, limiting their scalability. We propose SynerTCD, a two-stage framework that combines the efficiency of CNNs with the accuracy of VLMs through confidence-aware routing. In the first stage, a lightweight CNN processes each image and produces a calibrated confidence score. A routing module forwards only uncertain samples to a VLM for semantic verification in the second stage. Three key innovations enable this design: (i) a calibration-aware CNN training procedure with a dynamic loss selector and a novel Top-Accuracy Calibration Error (TACE) loss that aligns confidence with correctness; (ii) a TAC score based adaptive threshold finder automatically determines the optimal routing boundary for each content category without manual tuning; and (iii) a structured, heatmap-guided prompt template that improves VLM accuracy by 19.4% over baseline prompts. Evaluated on over 207,000 images across nine toxic categories and twelve baselines, SynerTCD reduces inference latency by 87.3% compared to standalone Qwen3-VL32B while achieving comparable or superior F1 scores on seven categories. Under equal VLM scale, SynerTCD achieves 81% faster inference with +0.5% higher accuracy. Ablation studies confirm the contribution of each component. SynerTCD offers a practical and effective solution for real-world content moderation.

Abstract
Detecting toxic visual content on social media demands both high accuracy and low latency. While Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) offer fast inference, they struggle with semantically complex images containing context, symbols, or embedded text. Conversely, Vision-Language Models (VLMs) provide strong semantic reasoning but incur 10-100× higher computational costs, limiting their scalability. We propose SynerTCD, a two-stage framework that combines the efficiency of CNNs with the accuracy of VLMs through confidence-aware routing. In the first stage, a lightweight CNN processes each image and produces a calibrated confidence score. A routing module forwards only uncertain samples to a VLM for semantic verification in the second stage. Three key innovations enable this design: (i) a calibration-aware CNN training procedure with a dynamic loss selector and a novel Top-Accuracy Calibration Error (TACE) loss that aligns confidence with correctness; (ii) a TAC score based adaptive threshold finder automatically determines the optimal routing boundary for each content category without manual tuning; and (iii) a structured, heatmap-guided prompt template that improves VLM accuracy by 19.4% over baseline prompts. Evaluated on over 207,000 images across nine toxic categories and twelve baselines, SynerTCD reduces inference latency by 87.3% compared to standalone Qwen3-VL-32B while achieving comparable or superior F1 scores on seven categories. Under equal VLM scale, SynerTCD achieves 81% faster inference with +0.5% higher accuracy. Ablation studies confirm the contribution of each component. SynerTCD offers a practical and effective solution for real-world content moderation.

1. Introduction
With the rapid evolution of social media platforms, user-generated visual content has experienced explosive growth. On platforms such as Instagram, TikTok, X (formerly Twitter), and Reddit, a volume of images and videos is uploaded, shared, and downloaded every minute. This content includes various forms of "toxic content" ranging from pornography, horror, and drugrelated material to gambling advertisements and political extremism. When such toxic content is not detected and removed promptly, the consequences for society can be far reaching. Violence becomes normalized, radical political narratives gain momentum, and minors are exposed to material unsuitable for their age. Regulatory

frameworks such as the European Union's Digital Services Act now mandate that content hosting providers detect and remove illegal or toxic material within hours of notification [1]. Thus, the development of efficient and accurate automated systems for moderating toxic content has emerged as a critical, practical necessity for all media platforms. Deploying such systems at scale presents two challenges. The first is detection scale: the volume of content requiring inspection is enormous, and the screening must happen in real time. The second is semantic complexity: toxic content takes highly diverse forms, and its meaning is often deeply tied to context. For instance, a photograph of a political rally might be entirely innocuous within one cultural context yet highly inflammatory in another; similarly, images depicting drug paraphernalia can sometimes be virtually indistinguishable from ordinary household items. To successfully navigate these two challenges, it is es-

sential to develop capabilities that can adapt the depth of their analysis to the specific scene and content of each sample.
Over the past decade, deep learning has fundamentally reshaped the field of visual content moderation. Within this domain, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have become the backbone of image classification due to their powerful hierarchical feature extraction capabilities. Foundational architectures such as ResNet [2] and EfficientNet [3] established robust baselines through residual learning and principled compound scaling, while lightweight variants such as MobileNetV3 [4] brought these capabilities to resource constrained environments through depthwise separable convolutions [5]. ConvNeXt [6] further advanced the industrial application of CNN. Concurrently, Researchers have also developed practical remedies for severe class imbalance, including Focal Loss reweighting [7] and targeted data augmentation [8]. As illustrated in Figure 1, CNNs are highly efficient and excel at recognizing explicit visual patterns such as flames or firearms, their inherently local receptive fields limit their ability to capture global context, symbolic meaning, and cultural nuance. These limitations become especially pronounced in tasks that demand deep semantic understanding, such as political content analysis [9] and extremist speech detection [10], where the meaning of an image arises not from any single visual pattern but from the interaction of symbols, text, and situational context.
Vision Language Models have opened a fundamentally different avenue by combining visual perception with textual reasoning [11, 12, 13]. By jointly analyzing image content, embedded text, and situational context, VLMs achieve significantly higher accuracy on semantically ambiguous samples [14] and can generate natural language explanations that support interpretable moderation decisions. Recent models such as Qwen3VL [15] and DeepSeek-VL2 [16] have further advanced fine grained perception and reasoning across multiple steps. However, the powerful semantic analysis ability of VLMs comes at a substantial computational cost, the inference time of VLMs is one to two orders of magnitude higher than that of CNNs, making them unsuitable as the only classifier in high throughput pipelines.
Meanwhile, existing research on rapid response engineering has focused primarily on general-purpose benchmarks, leaving the specific adaptability to the often subtle semantic features characteristic of toxic content unexplored. Confidence calibration methods have been designed around single fixed models with fixed global thresholds, where the cost of missing a toxic

To address these challenges, we propose SynerTCD, a framework for Synergistic Toxic Content Detection via confidence-aware routing between CNN and VLM. Building upon the collaborative paradigm introduced in BTCD [18], SynerTCD advances the framework in three key dimensions: (i) a calibration-aware CNN training procedure with a dynamic loss selector and a confidence-routing calibration loss that explicitly aligns confidence scores with prediction correctness; (ii) a TAC score based adaptive threshold finder that automatically determines category-specific routing boundaries, replacing the fixed global threshold; and (iii) a structured four component prompt template guided by CNN-generated heatmaps, which systematically improves VLM detection accuracy by 19.4 percentage points over category name only prompts. The framework is built on the observation that not every image requires the same depth of analysis: a lightweight CNN can handle the vast majority of samples at minimal cost, while the more expensive reasoning power of a VLM should be reserved for genuinely ambiguous cases.
SynerTCD operates as a two-stage online pipeline. In

2

the first stage, a frozen CNN backbone paired with a lightweight MLP classification head processes each input image, produces a detection label together with a calibrated confidence score pi. A routing module then compares this score against an adaptive threshold . When the CNN is confident in its prediction, the result is accepted directly as the final output. When the CNN is uncertain, the sample is forwarded to the second stage, where a VLM refiner performs fine grained semantic verification and generates a natural language explanation for its inferences.
We validate SynerTCD on a benchmark of ten datasets covering one normal category and nine toxic categories, totaling over 207,000 images, and compare it against twelve baselines spanning lightweight CNNs, heavyweight CNNs, Vision Transformers, and VLMs ranging from 0.5B to 32B parameters. SynerTCD matches or exceeds the F1 scores of 32B parameter models on the vast majority of categories while requiring only about 12% of their inference time. In a challenging ten class scenario, SynerTCD reduces inference latency by 8.8% relative to the standalone VLM while simultaneously improving accuracy by 6 percentage points. Ablation studies confirm that each proposed component contributes meaningfully to these results.
The main contributions of this paper are as follows:
· We propose SynerTCD, a cascaded CNN-VLM framework for toxic content detection that routes samples based on calibrated confidence, achieving 81% lower average latency than 32B VLMs while maintaining state-of-the-art accuracy.
· We propose a calibration-aware CNN training method using a dynamic loss selector and a TopAccuracy Calibration Error (TACE) loss that penalizes overconfident errors and maximizes error recall in low-confidence regions.
· We design an adaptive threshold optimization mechanism using a TAC score that automatically determines per-category routing thresholds, eliminating manual tuning and improving crosscategory robustness.
· We develop a structured, heatmap-guided prompt template for VLMs, incorporating role assignment, task decomposition, and conservative bias. This strategy improves VLM accuracy by 19.4 percentage points and remains robust across temperature settings and multi-class scenarios.

2. Related work
2.1. CNN-based Toxic Content Detection
Deep learning has transformed visual content moderation, with Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) becoming the dominant paradigm owing to their powerful feature extraction capabilities. Foundational architectures such as ResNet [2] and EfficientNet [3] established strong baselines through residual learning and principled compound scaling, respectively. Lightweight variants, notably MobileNetV3 [4], extended these advances to resource-constrained platforms via depthwise separable convolutions [5], while ConvNeXt [6] modernized the convolutional paradigm by incorporating Transformer-inspired design choices such as inverted bottlenecks and large kernels. Beyond architecture design, the field has matured toward multi-task formulations that jointly predict fine-grained toxicity categories through shared feature extractors with taskspecific heads, alongside techniques for addressing severe class imbalance, including data augmentation [8] and focal loss re-weighting [7].
However, CNNs' inherently local receptive fields limit their ability to understand global context, symbolic meaning, and cultural nuances. This is particularly evident in tasks requiring deep semantic understanding, such as political content analysis [9]. Although some work has explored extremist speech detection [10] and sentiment analysis [19], CNNs still struggle to accurately classify symbols and behaviors that depend on complex social contexts.
2.2. VLM-based Toxic Content Detection
Vision-Language Models have opened a new paradigm for toxic content detection. CLIP [11] demonstrated that aligning visual and textual embeddings enables zero-shot classification against toxic content descriptors. Generative VLMs advanced this further: BLIP-2 [12] introduced query-based bootstrapping to bridge image encoders with language models; LLaVA [13] and MiniGPT-4 [20] showed that visual instruction

3

tuning produces natural-language explanations valuable for interpretable moderation [14]. More recent architectures including FastVLM [21], DeepSeek-VL2 [16], and Qwen3 [15] have continued to advance efficient alignment, high-resolution perception, and finegrained semantic understanding. Hierarchical Vision Transformers such as Swin Transformer [22] have been widely adopted as visual encoders within VLM pipelines. Beyond detection, VLMs have also been applied to safety-aware generation control [1, 23, 24].
Despite their semantic understanding capabilities, VLMs' computational cost is typically one to two orders of magnitude higher than that of CNNs, which hinders their large-scale deployment. VLMs excel at handling samples that rely on subtle semantic cues, which are challenging for CNNs [25], but their high cost is unnecessary for samples with clear features. Existing research on efficient VLM adaptation [26], confidence calibration [27, 28], and multimodal content analysis [29] provides a foundation for our work.
2.3. Prompt Engineering for VLMs
How a VLM is prompted has proven as consequential as the model's architecture. Learnable softprompt methods formalized this insight early: CoOp [30] showed that optimizing continuous prompt vectors outperforms hand-crafted templates; CoCoOp [31] extended this by conditioning prompts on individual inputs; and MaPLe [32] introduced multi-modal tuning across both vision and language branches. Visual Prompt Tuning [33] demonstrated that prepending learnable tokens to Vision Transformer inputs is an effective adaptation strategy. A parallel, training-free line of research pursued prompt enrichment through LLMgenerated visual descriptions as richer textual anchors [34, 35, 36] and few-shot adaptation without parameter updates [37]. At the reasoning level, Chain-of-Thought prompting [38] has been adapted to VLMs for multistep safety classification, guiding models through scene description, toxic element identification, and toxicity labeling.
A persistent challenge is prompt sensitivity: minor lexical variations can produce drastically different predictions, which is unacceptable in safety-critical settings [30, 31, 35]. Moreover, most prompt engineering research targets general-purpose benchmarks, and its adaptation to the nuanced semantics of toxic content remains largely underexplored.

2.4. Confidence Calibration and Uncertainty Estimation
Well-calibrated confidence scores are essential for systems that must assess trust in their own predictions, especially in toxic content detection. Temperature scaling [27] remains the most widely used post-hoc technique, though modern architectures exhibit qualitatively different miscalibration patterns, tending toward under confidence on in-distribution data and overconfidence on out-of-distribution inputs [17]. Focal loss offers a complementary training-time approach by implicitly improving calibration through down-weighting easy examples [7]. In the VLM domain, CLIP's similarity scores are poorly calibrated as class probabilities, and generative VLMs' verbalized confidence correlates only weakly with actual accuracy [39]. Post-hoc multimodal calibration has been explored but degrades under temporal and demographic shifts inherent to evolving toxic content.
Selective prediction has gained traction as a principled uncertainty-handling strategy. Galil and El-Yaniv [40] studied selective prediction across ImageNet classifiers, while Tian et al. [41] formalized routing uncertain samples to oracles rather than risking incorrect decisions. A common limitation of existing methods is that they apply calibration to a single monolithic model with a fixed global threshold, which is insufficient for toxic content detection where cost asymmetry between false positives and false negatives is extreme and data distributions shift continuously.
Several established paradigms address the trade-off between accuracy and efficiency through staged or conditional inference. Conventional model cascades often arrange models of increasing capacity in sequence and forward a sample to later stages when the current model is insufficiently confident, typically to trade accuracy against computation. Selective prediction allows a model to abstain on low-confidence samples and evaluates risk over the covered samples, leaving abstained cases unresolved unless an additional deferral mechanism is introduced. Early-exit networks typically attach auxiliary classifiers to intermediate layers of a single backbone and terminate inference once an internal prediction is confident enough, thereby reducing the average computational depth. Oracle-routing methods often assume a perfect second-stage expert, while some human-deferral methods model the human as a highly reliable or external decision-maker; in such settings, routed cases are commonly treated as corrected or improved. Unlike standard cascades that often use a global confidence rule to trade accuracy for computation, SynerTCD learns or calibrates confidence estimates for

4

routing and applies category-specific thresholds tailored to the error patterns of toxic categories. Unlike selective prediction, which abstains on low-confidence samples, SynerTCD routes uncertain cases to a vision-language model for refinement and still produces a final decision. Unlike early-exit networks, which reduce computation by exiting early within a single backbone, SynerTCD routes between heterogeneous experts, combining fast visual screening with deeper semantic reasoning. Finally, unlike oracle-routing methods, or deferral settings that treat the second-stage expert as near-perfect, the vision-language model in SynerTCD can both correct and introduce errors, and the evaluation reflects the actual routed outputs. The novelty therefore lies not in the generic use of a two-stage pipeline, but in the taskspecific integration of calibrated confidence estimation, category-specific routing thresholds, and semantic verification for toxic content detection.
In summary, existing approaches face a fundamental tension between the efficiency of CNN classifiers and the semantic reasoning capability of VLMs, while prompt engineering and confidence calibration research have yet to be jointly leveraged within a unified detection framework. The SynerTCD framework proposed in this paper addresses this efficiency-accuracy dilemma by cascading a calibrated CNN and VLM, routing samples adaptively based on prediction confidence so that expensive semantic reasoning is invoked only when genuinely needed, thereby achieving high accuracy while substantially reducing computational overhead.

For a candidate threshold, rerr denotes the fraction of CNN errors that fall into the low-confidence region and are therefore routed to the VLM. s = 1 - rvlm denotes the fraction of samples handled by the CNN alone, serving as a proxy for saved VLM computation. By jointly rewarding CNN-error capture and VLM savings,

TAC Score discourages two degenerate routing policies. One accepts nearly every CNN output, yielding high savings but low error recall because few CNN mistakes are routed for review. The score weights error capture more heavily than efficiency, reflecting the assumption that an unreviewed toxic-content error is more costly than an additional VLM call. For each category, the threshold is selected on a validation set by maximizing TAC Score over candidate thresholds, yielding a category-specific operating point that balances reliability and computational cost.

3.4. VLM Prompt Engineering
Toxic content detection via VLMs is challenging for two reasons: (1) semantic ambiguity toxic indicators can be subtle or context-dependent, making simple binary prompts insufficient; and (2) false-negative bias general-purpose VLMs tend toward conservative predictions and may miss genuinely toxic content. To address these issues, we design a structured prompt template comprising four components (T1-T4), whose individual contributions are validated in the ablation study.
We focus on enhancing VLM's ability to handle complex toxic content detection, ensuring it can accurately follow user instructions. Prompts are fine-tuned to improve VLM's responsiveness to detailed user prompts and complex analysis.
3.4.1. High-Contribution Location Analysis Based on the trained CNN model, we generate pixel-
level heatmaps via LayerCAM [42] for representative high-confidence and low-confidence samples. These heatmaps highlight regions with the highest contribution to the CNN's decision. The salient regions are then converted into natural-language object descriptions, providing spatially grounded cues. Compared with zero-shot or few-shot strategies that rely solely on category names, this heatmap guided approach captures fine-grained spatial information critical for detecting subtle toxic content.

8

3.4.2. Prompt Template Design
To address the uncertainty and diversity of toxic objects in images, we adaptively generate object-specific prompts from diverse textual templates, effectively tackling the challenge of accurately describing semantically complex toxic content. This prompt enables the model to more precisely capture and describe toxic content.
T1: Toxic Content Category. We incorporate the category of toxic content as part of the prompt, such as "Accident", "Drug".
T2: Role Assignment. We establish the VLM as a specialized expert in toxic content detection, aligning its output distribution with the detection task. Without an explicit role definition, the VLM's evaluation boundaries may be inconsistent across categories. The prompt begins with a clearly defined role, , "You are an expert in toxic content detection".
T3: Task Transformation. Rather than posing detection as a single binary question, we decompose it into a hierarchical classification and object recognition task that evaluates multiple semantic dimensions. In addition to identifying objects in the image, the VLM is guided to analyze contextual and linguistic cues. Taking political content detection as an illustrative example, the hierarchy comprises four primary categories: (1) Extremist Symbols and Messaging (hate symbols, inflammatory slogans, and discriminatory content), (2) Incitement and Radicalization (calls for violence, polarizing rhetoric, and mobilization tactics), (3) Cultural and Religious Sensitivity (offensive representations, misuse of sacred symbols, and cultural appropriation), and (4) Context and Intent Analysis (gathering purposes, crowd composition, and underlying political motivations). This structured approach guides VLMs to evaluate content across multiple semantic dimensions, ensuring comprehensive coverage of both explicit and implicit toxic indicators.
T4: Conservative Bias. The fourth component embeds a safety-first principle aimed at minimizing false negative. This explicit instruction shifts the VLM's decision boundary toward higher recall, which is desirable because missing genuinely toxic content incurs far greater cost than a false alarm. Moreover, since the CNN classifier primarily relies on visual patterns, the conservative bias encourages the VLM to complement the CNN by leveraging its broader world knowledge for contextual and situational reasoning, thereby reducing the overall false-negative rate.

4. Evaluations
4.1. Experiment Setting
Unless otherwise specified, the same experimental setup and equipment were utilized to evaluate the performance of SynerTCD.
Datasets. We evaluate SynerTCD on ten curated datasets, which are summarized in Table 1. All data were preprocessed and cleaned from original sources to ensure quality and consistency, including duplicate removal, quality filtering, and annotation verification. For each toxic category, we form a binary detection task by pairing its samples with the Normal category, then randomly split the data into training, validation, and test sets at an 8:1:1 ratio without reusing samples across splits. The Normal samples assigned to each task are drawn at random, so their distribution differs across categories. Only the training and validation splits are used during CNN Training Phase, Adaptive Confidence Threshold Finder and VLM Prompt Engineering. The test split is held out and reserved exclusively for final evaluation. Detailed toxic category definitions and the full dataset processing cleaning protocol are provided in Appendix A.
Evaluation Metrics. We report Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1 score for detection quality, Average Inference Time (ms) for efficiency, and VLM Call Rate (VCR, %) for the fraction of samples routed to Stage II. Lower VCR indicates more efficient use of the VLM. All metrics except Time and VCR are reported in %.
Implementation Details: All models are implemented in PyTorch (v1.13) with Python 3.10 on Ubuntu 22.04, running on an Intel® Xeon® Gold 6226R CPU and two NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU with CUDA 12.9 and

9

driver version 575.57.08. We employed linear probing CNN backbones using transfer learning with frozen feature extractors and train only final classification layers. CNNs use SGD (lr=0.001, momentum=0.9), while Swin Transformer uses AdamW (lr=1e-4) with gradient clipping and AMP. CLIP (ViT-B/32) employs linear probing with AdamW (lr=1e-4). All models are trained on 224×224 images with standard data augmentation, and train = 0.85. CNN training uses a batch size of 128, while all models perform sample-by-sample inference to ensure a consistent and fair measurement of perimage latency. We select MobileNetV3 as our CNN backbone for its computational efficiency and competitive accuracy performance as demonstrated, making it suitable for the first-stage screening task in our cascaded framework. To enable a comparative analysis of the impact of VLM parameter size on accuracy and inference time, experiments in Section 4.2.1 are conducted using both Qwen3-VL-8B and Qwen3-VL-32B as the VLM refiner. For the ablation studies in Section 5, Qwen3VL-8B is employed as the VLM refiner.
For VLM inference, all models are served through the vLLM framework using their native non-quantized precision. Request-level inference caching is disabled so that reported latencies reflect actual per-sample computation rather than reuse of previous results. The sampling temperature is set to 0.02, selected according to the sensitivity analysis in Table 7, with top_p = 0.9. We do not impose an additional task-specific generation cap beyond the maximum generation length, which is set to 4096 tokens. Since inference is performed locally, any generation failure triggers automatic re-inference until a well-formed and definitive result is obtained. The prompt template imposes explicit constraints on the output format, requiring the VLM to conclude with a clear YES or NO decision. The final label is then extracted directly from this constrained output. For confidence calibration, Expected Calibration Error (ECE) is computed using 15 equal-mass bins. The deployment-time routing threshold is selected on the validation set following Eq. 12. Specifically, the trained CNN is applied to the validation split, and its confidence scores, predicted labels, and ground-truth labels are used to compute the TAC Score over candidate thresholds and determine the optimal per-category threshold. Ground-truth labels are used only for validation-time threshold selection and are not used during test-time routing. To ensure a fair comparison, standalone VLM baselines and the VLM stage of SynerTCD use matched prompt templates with the same output constraints, decoding configuration, and latency measurement protocol.

Qwen3-VL-32B [15] scales the Qwen3-VL architecture to 32 B parameters, leveraging the expanded capacity to concurrently capture fine-grained visual details and complex cross-modal semantic relationships, yielding state-of-the-art performance on diverse visionlanguage benchmarks.
4.2. Experiment Results
4.2.1. Overall Toxic Content Detection
In this section, SynerTCD employs MobileNetV3Large as the backbone for the CNN classifier in Stage 1, Qwen3-VL-8B and Qwen3-VL-32B as the VLM refiner in Stage 2, balancing the efficiency advantage of the CNN model with the accuracy advantage of the VLM. Table 2 shows the fundamental trade-off between efficiency and semantic understanding in toxic content detection.
The Limits of CNNs. CNN models offer high inference efficiency but lack classification accuracy on complex samples. MobileNetV3-Large achieves the fastest average latency of 6.08 ms per sample and obtains a strong F1 score of 95.1% on the "porn" category. However, constrained by its limited model capacity, the overall accuracy remains low, with a average F1 of only 89.3% across the nine toxic categories. Across 10 runs for each backbone and category, the standard deviation of the test metrics remains below 0.05. The small variance in test performance indicates that the CNN stage is robust to random initialization, data shuffling, and other stochastic factors. The reported averages are consistent and a backbone can be chosen from the accuracy and latency trade-off alone. In addition, the average improvement in ACC and F1 from the frozen-backbone setting to the unfrozen-backbone setting is less than 0.5%, suggesting that the pretrained backbone features are already well aligned with the current toxic-content detection

task.Even under this stronger unfrozen-backbone setting, SynerTCD remains superior to all evaluated baselines, supporting the conclusion that its advantage is not merely due to a stronger CNN stage but to the synergistic design of the overall framework. Full results are provided in Appendix C.
The Limits of VLMs. VLM models achieve high recognition accuracy on complex samples, especially those requiring joint analysis of symbols, messaging, context, and intent. For example, "political" samples contain numerous politically related slogans and banners. CNN models struggle with such classification tasks because they lack Optical Character Recognition (OCR) and sensitive text recognition capabilities, whereas VLMs integrate semantics, textual content, and scene context to produce the final classification, thus achieving higher accuracy. Larger VLMs yield higher accuracy at the cost of slower inference. Specifically, Qwen3-VL-8B achieves a average accuracy of 96.5% with an average latency of 1842.4 ms, while Qwen3VL-32B achieves a average accuracy of 97.2% with an average latency of 2754.5 ms.
SynerTCD Performance. SynerTCD achieves the best performance across the majority of toxic content categories. Notably, on semantically complex categories such as "political", "robbery", and "weapon", SynerTCD (Qwen3-VL-32B) attains ACC exceeding 98%, significantly outperforming all baseline methods. Compared to the standalone Qwen3-VL-32B model, SynerTCD (Qwen3-VL-8B) achieves higher average ACC and F1 scores while requiring only 12% of the inference time. In terms of detection latency, both configurations of SynerTCD consistently outperform the standalone VLM. Although SynerTCD (Qwen3-VL-8B) exhibits a marginal decrease of 0.4% in average ACC compared to SynerTCD (Qwen3-VL-32B), it delivers a 33% improvement in detection speed.
An exception is observed in the drug category, where SynerTCD achieves an F1 score of only 94.0% with a VCR of 17.022.85%, which is notably lower than the performance of the standalone Qwen3-VL-8B (ACC = 95.4%) and Qwen3-VL-32B (ACC = 96.2%). Upon qualitative analysis, we observe that the CNN classifier tends to misclassify drug-related images as normal with high confidence. This occurs because many drugrelated visuals figures resemble ordinary objects (e.g., pills in bottles, powders in bags), leading the CNN to assign high confidence to incorrect predictions, which are consequently never routed to the VLM for verification. 4.2.2. Analysis of the Accuracy-Efficiency Trade-off
To further investigate the impact of the confidence threshold  on system performance, we compare the performance under different confidence thresholds, with MobileNetV3-Large as the CNN classifier and Qwen3VL-8B as the VLM refiner.
Table 3 presents the test results on the "accident" dataset. As the confidence threshold increases, the VLM call rate also increases. Consequently, the accuracy of SynerTCD first rises, reaches a peak, and then begins to decline, eventually converging to the accuracy of the VLM alone. When  = 0.85, SynerTCD reaches the optimal balance point and achieves the highest F1 score of 98.1%. Beyond this threshold, although the VLM Call Rate increases substantially to 20.4%, the marginal performance gain diminishes and even slightly decreases. The initial accuracy improvement is because the VLM is overall more accurate than the CNN. The subsequent accuracy decline is due to samples that the CNN classifies correctly with high confidence but that the VLM misclassifies. As the VCR increases, the VLM reviews more of these high-confidence samples and incorrectly overrules them, leading to a drop in accuracy.
This validates the effectiveness of the proposed SynerTCD. Although large VLMs excel in accuracy, their inference cost is prohibitively high. In contrast, SynerTCD invokes the VLM only when necessary through the confidence-aware routing mechanism, controlling the average inference time while maintaining or even surpassing the accuracy of large VLMs.

5.1.5. Adaptive Confidence Threshold Finder Analysis
A fixed global threshold often fails to adapt to dynamic variations in data distributions. Our adaptive finder scans the confidence distribution and computes the TAC score to balance the overall system accuracy and latency. We define VLM review of samples that the CNN correctly classifies as ineffective review, and VLM review of samples that the CNN misclassifies as effective review.
Figure 6 illustrates the relationship between the confidence threshold  and both the TAC score and the Capture Error Recall for MobileNetV3-Large trained with the TACE loss across the nine categories. The optimal TAC scores are achieved when  is set to [0.835, 0.75, 0.785, 0.835, 0.785, 0.695, 0.85, 0.72, 0.745] for the nine toxic content detection CNN models. At these thresholds, the Capture error recall has already risen close to its maximum in every category, which indicates that selecting the optimal  by the TAC score alone captures almost all of the recoverable errors routes a large fraction of first-stage CNN errors without requiring a separate recall objective. Consistent with the observations in Table 3, as the confidence threshold increases, the TAC score first rises, reaches a peak, and then declines, eventually dropping to zero. This is because, as  increases, the VLM call rate also increases, and more samples pass through Stage 2 VLM refiner. A greater number of effective reviews raises the scores of both high and low. However, beyond the optimal threshold, a large number of correctly classified CNN samples are still sent for VLM review, resulting in substantial ineffective reviews that rapidly lower the overall TAC score.

5.2. VLM Prompts Sensitivity Analysis
The prompt engineering strategy for VLMs directly affects their depth of understanding of toxic content. We analyze this from three dimensions: High-Contribution Location, prompt template, and prompt temperature.

5.2.1. High-Contribution Location Analysis
After processing by LayerCAM [42], the CNN model produces a contribution map indicating how much each pixel contributes to the model's inference. Figure 7 visualizes the original images, the high-contribution pixel locations, and the composite overlay through heatmaps. It can be observed that different toxic categories exhibit distinct attention patterns. For example, the "fire" category shows high pixel contributions in regions containing flames, the "weapon" category shows high contributions in regions containing firearms, and the "robbery" category shows high contributions in regions associated with scene layout and interpersonal relationships. Performing object and context analysis on these high-contribution regions enables better optimization of VLM prompts, further improving VLM accuracy.
Each category prompt is built through a four-stage interactive optimization with GPT-5, detailed in Appendix F.1. In this process, GPT-5 is used as a promptconstruction assistant to generate a structured category definition, rewrite it using an expert role and a conservative decision default, ground the description in concrete

16

visual evidence from 100 training/validation images with LayerCAM-highlighted CNN-salient regions, and finalize the prompt with explicit output constraints.This process converts broad category names into more precise, evidence-grounded prompts with explicit visual decision criteria. By incorporating visual evidence associated with the toxic signal, the resulting prompts help the VLM more effectively evaluate the low-confidence samples routed by the CNN.
5.2.2. Prompts Comparison Analysis We design a structured prompt template comprising
five components: H for manual, T1 for toxic category definition, T2 for role assignment, T3 for task decomposition, and T4 for conservative bias.
Table 6 presents the impact of different component combinations on the average performance across the nine categories. Using T1 alone yields an average ACC of only 77.3%, indicating that toxic category definition alone is insufficient for handling complex scenes. Adding T2 (role assignment) raises the average ACC to 83.8%, demonstrating that establishing the VLM as a domain expert meaningfully aligns its outputs with the detection task. Further incorporating T3 (task decomposition) brings the ACC to 93.2%, confirming that hierarchical multi-dimensional classification substantially deepens VLM understanding. When all four components T1, T2, T3, and T4 are enabled simultaneously, the system achieves the highest average ACC of 96.7% and the highest average F1 of 96.5%. In particular, the inclusion of T3 and T4 significantly reduces the miss rate, ensuring the capture of subtle toxic content. This validates the effectiveness of our proposed structured prompt template in guiding VLMs for deep semantic reasoning. In contrast, the majority-voting scheme lets each binary classifier vote either for its toxic category or for Normal. Normal is selected when it receives the majority of votes; otherwise, among the toxic-positive classifiers, the final toxic category is chosen using the highest toxic confidence. This raises accuracy to 68.0% and F1 to 44.9%, with a small CNN-side latency of 6.90 ms per sample, which is negligible relative to VLM inference time. It also substantially outperforms the directly trained ten-class CNN evaluated under the same protocol, which achieves 33% accuracy.
Because the voting scheme reuses the confidence scores of the stronger binary classifiers, it preserves a useful confidence signal for routing. Empirically, applying SynerTCD on top of this voting scheme improves overall accuracy to 86.9% and F1 to 82.7%. Crucially, without modifying the VLM refiner, this strategy reduces the VLM Call Rate from 91.2% to 49.7%. Under the same latency measurement protocol, including the parallel binary CNN stage, voting overhead, and all VLM calls, the average inference time decreases to 990.86 ms, roughly half that of the direct ten-class SynerTCD configuration and the standalone VLM baseline.
These results indicate that decomposing the multiclass task into parallel binary classifications substantially improves the discriminative strength of the CNN stage and makes confidence-aware routing effective again. The parallel binary voting scheme improves CNN-side classification relative to direct ten-class training, while the resulting confidence scores allow SynerTCD to reduce unnecessary VLM calls and recover

a favorable accuracy-latency trade-off. Overall, these findings suggest that SynerTCD remains effective and computationally practical in more complex multi-class moderation settings, substantially outperforming the direct ten-class CNN and standalone VLM baselines and supporting its applicability to practical contentmoderation workflows.
6. Conclusion
In this study, we presented SynerTCD, a cascaded framework that synergistically combines the efficiency of CNN classifiers with the semantic reasoning capability of Vision-Language Models for toxic content detection. The framework introduces three key technical contributions: (i) a calibration-aware CNN training phase with a dynamic loss selector and a differentiable TACE loss that aligns confidence scores with actual prediction correctness; (ii) an adaptive threshold finder based on TAC score optimization that automatically determines category-specific routing thresholds; and (iii) a structured four component prompt template (T1-T4) that guides VLMs toward comprehensive, recall-oriented toxic content analysis. Experiments across nine toxic content categories and over 207,000 images demonstrate that SynerTCD (Qwen3-VL-8B) achieves F1 scores comparable to or exceeding 32B VLMs on seven of nine categories while reducing inference time by approximately 87.3%. Ablation studies confirm the contribution of each component and the robustness of the prompt strategy to temperature settings.

19

7. Limitations and Future Work
Several limitations of the proposed approach are noted: The framework is constrained by highconfidence errors from the CNN. For instance, in the drug category, the Adaptive Confidence Threshold Finder offers significant speedup but provides limited accuracy gains, failing to fully utilize the VLM's potential to rectify these mistakes. In certain binary classification scenarios, the smaller VLM refiner may perform worse than the initial CNN, which can result in the VLM adversely overriding a correct CNN prediction. The CNN classifier exhibits significant performance degradation in multiclass toxic content detection scenario, causing most samples to be forwarded to the VLM. As a result, SynerTCD achieves only an 8.8% reduction in average inference time compared to the standalone VLM. We regard this multi-class setting as a stress test, where mutually exclusive labels, mixed semantics, and the dominant proportion of Normal samples can weaken the ability of a single ten-class CNN selector to separate reliable from error-prone predictions, thereby increasing the VCR. Replacing the single selector with multiple binary CNN selectors combined through simple voting improves this confidence discrimination and substantially lowers the VCR. This suggests that the current version of SynerTCD is better suited to category-specific binary screening or hierarchical moderation pipelines than to directly replacing a general-purpose multi-class verifier.

Appendix A. DataSet Defination and Preprocessing
Appendix A.1. Toxic Content Defination
For the purpose of establishing a standardized taxonomy for sensitive-content moderation, annotators performed independent binary classification for each of the following nine categories. Labels are assigned based on visible content rather than broader intent or context. Thus, fictional, documentary, artistic, or educational depictions are labeled positive if the visual evidence satisfies the corresponding definition.
Accident. Definition: Content documenting a failure event resulting in overturned vehicles, collapsed structures, or substantial damage to public infrastructure. Positive examples: A passenger vehicle resting on its roof or side after a collision; a vehicle breaching a guardrail or colliding with a building facade; a derailed train carriage; severe cracks, collapse, or subsidence on a bridge or roadway caused by a catastrophic event.
Drug. Definition: Content depicting recognizable narcotics, psychotropic substances, or associated paraphernalia signaling illegal use. Positive examples: Cannabis buds, methamphetamine, heroin or cocaine powder, ecstasy tablets, syringes, glass pipes, or other drug-use equipment.
Fire. Definition: Content visually documenting burning and damage caused by fire. Positive examples: Residential or commercial structures with active flames breaching windows or roofs; vehicles engulfed in fire; scenes with heavy smoke and visible fire damage. Candles, fireplaces, and campfires.
Gambling. Definition: Content depicting monetary wagering activities or equipment specifically designed for games of chance and betting. Positive examples: Playing cards on a casino table with visible betting chips; dice on a craps layout; Mahjong with visible monetary stakes; slot-machine screens or online betting interfaces showing active wagering.
Horror. Definition: Content with the primary intent to induce fear, shock through bodily mutilation, or supernatural entities. Positive examples: Realistic or

stylized depictions of severe injuries with exposed tissue; dismembered body parts; decaying corpses; phantoms; monstrous humanoids.
Political. Definition: Content depicting organized collective political action or the physical artifacts of such action in public spaces. Positive examples: Mass gatherings carrying banners or placards with political slogans; street marches, sit-ins, or rallies; posters, banners, graffiti, or signs expressing overt political messaging.
Porn. Definition: Content featuring exposed human or anthropomorphic genitalia, or explicitly depicting sexual intercourse, masturbation, or other overt sexual acts. Positive examples: Direct visual representation of the penis, vulva, anus, or realistic prosthetic replicas thereof in a sexual context; explicit depictions of sexual acts.
Robbery. Definition: Content depicting an act of theft involving the application or explicit threat of force against persons in a private or commercial setting. Positive examples: A perpetrator brandishing a weapon to demand valuables from a cashier or victim; mugging scenes; forced surrender of money, phones, bags, or other valuables.
Weapon. Definition: Content depicting purposebuilt instruments designed for causing harm, existing independently of violent behavioral context. Positive examples: Firearms, combat knives, swords, explosive devices, or other purpose-built instruments designed to injure or kill.

Appendix B. Resource of Different Models
Table A.9 compares the resource usage of three model families. GPU memory for Qwen3-VL-32B. This gap of more than one order of magnitude explains why a large VLM cannot serve as an always-on classifier. It also motivates SynerTCD, which uses a lightweight CNN as the default predictor and invokes the large VLM only for selected low-confidence samples, thereby reducing average VLM computation and latency while retaining VLM-level reasoning for difficult cases.
Appendix C. CNN Training Phase Analysis
Appendix C.1. Stability Analysis of CNN Backbones
Figure B.10 reports the stability of the four CNN backbones used in SynerTCD. For each of the nine toxic categories, every backbone is trained with tace for 10 independent runs, and we summarize the average ACC, F1, and inference Time together with the variance across runs. First, the across-run variability of test accuracy and F1 remains very small for every backbone and category, and the error bars are barely visible in most subplots. This indicates that tace produces highly repeatable results and that the reported averages reflect consistent behavior rather than a few favorable runs. Second, the accuracy ranking of the backbones is preserved across all categories, with ResNet50, MobileNetV3-Large, and EfficientNet-B3 achieving comparable ACC and F1 while ConvNeXtTiny trails slightly. The stable accuracy together with the low variance confirms that tace offers backboneagnostic and category-agnostic robustness, allowing SynerTCD to select a backbone based on the accuracy and latency trade-off without concern for training instability.

Appendix D. High-Confidence Analysis
Appendix D.1. Qualitative Analysis of HighConfidence CNN Errors
Figure D.12 presents representative cases where the CNN phase is both wrong and highly confident across all nine toxic categories. The first two rows are false positives, in which normal content is reported as toxic, and the last two rows are false negatives, in which toxic content is missed and treated as normal. These examples expose the two failure modes that a confidenceonly threshold cannot catch. A false positive arises when a normal image shares low-level cues with a toxic category, for example a normal sample that contains pills or powder is reported as Drug because its color and texture resemble narcotics. A false negative arises when toxic content is presented in an atypical form or when the abnormal behavior is not visually salient, for example a Drug sample rendered as a comic or one in which the abnormal action is subtle is missed as normal. The same pattern holds for the other categories, where cluttered scenes, ambiguous objects, and stylized imagery drive confident yet incorrect predictions. Because the CNN confidence for the predicted label exceeds the corresponding routing threshold in each case, these errors survive confidence-only routing. Even so, the low selective risk observed under TACE indicates that such highconfidence errors are relatively rare in the CNN stage. Thus, the residual cases shown here represent rare highconfidence failures rather than typical CNN behavior. In this way, the CNN training and calibration strategy keeps the fraction of confidently wrong samples low, while SynerTCD routes mainly low-confidence cases to the VLM rather than invoking it on every sample. This yields an effective trade-off between overall accuracy and inference time.
Appendix D.2. Confidence Distrbutions of eight Toxic Content Categories
Figure D.13, Figure D.14, Figure D.15, Figure D.16, Figure D.17, Figure D.18, Figure D.19, Figure D.20 compare MobileNetV3-Large trained with TACE against the same backbone trained with cross-entropy. For each category, the figures show the confidence distributions of correct and incorrect predictions and the corresponding TAC score as a function of the routing threshold . Two effects are visible across the

figures. First,TACE yields a clearer empirical separation between correct and incorrect predictions than cross-entropy. In the TACE panels, correct predictions concentrate in the high-confidence bins, while incorrect predictions are pushed toward lower confidence, whereas the cross-entropy panels place most of the mass in the highest bin regardless of correctness, which makes a single threshold far less discriminative. Second, the optimal threshold varies with the difficulty of each category. Categories with sharper separation such as WeaponPorn with  = 0.85 and Accident with  = 0.835 tolerate a high threshold, while harder categories such as PornPolitical with  = 0.695 and Drug with  = 0.75 require a lower threshold to retain enough confident correct predictions. The dashed line in each figure marks the validation-set threshold that maximizes the TAC score. This point balances the reduction of highrisk CNN acceptances against the need to retain enough correct high-confidence predictions without unnecessarily increasing VLM calls. These distributions explain why the per-category thresholds used in SynerTCD differ across categories and why TACE is essential, since it produces the confidence structure that allows a threshold to route uncertain samples to the VLM while keeping confident CNN predictions in the fast path.

Threshold

Table E.10 compares nine threshold strategies with SynerTCD. BTCD [18] uses a Fixed Global Threshold with single shared confidence threshold for all nine categories. The threshold is selected on the validation set by maximizing the macro average TAC Score across the nine categories.
Fixed VCR is a budget constrained threshold selection strategy. For each category, it selects the largest confidence threshold that keeps the VCR within a predefined budget, where VCR is no greater than 15 percent. This method limits the number of samples routed to the VLM while preserving as much performance as possible.
Fixed Error Recall focuses on recalling CNN errors. For each category, it selects the smallest confidence threshold that satisfies the preset CNN error recall requirement, where Error Recall is no less than 80 percent. By using the minimum threshold that reaches the recall target, this method avoids unnecessary VLM calls while ensuring that a sufficient proportion of CNN mistakes can be reviewed.

Cost-based Optimization introduces a cost aware objective to balance accuracy and VLM usage. For each category, the threshold is selected by maximizing ACC - 0.1  VCR. This means that the method rewards higher routed accuracy while penalizing excessive VLM calls.
Uncertainty Estimators treats 1-Con f idence as the uncertainty score of CNN. It assumes that CNN errors tend to have higher uncertainty, and selects the threshold that maximizes T PR_errorFPR_correct. This method chooses the threshold that best separates erroneous CNN predictions from correct ones.
Ensemble Disagreement uses disagreement among multiple CNN models to decide which samples should be reviewed. Specifically, six TACELoss CNN models are used, and samples are sorted from high to low according to prediction disagreement. The number of reviewed samples is then selected to maximize TAC Score.
OOD (Out of Distribution) Detection uses additional out of distribution samples to estimate the threshold. For each binary classification model, 200 samples are randomly sampled from each of the other eight categories. The threshold is then determined by calculating TAC Score on these additional samples.
Class-Specific Safety Thresholds is a safety oriented threshold selection method. For each category, it selects the minimum threshold that simultaneously satisfies two safety constraints. The first constraint is that Error Recall is no less than 95%. The second constraint is that High Confidence False Negatives divided by positives is no greater than 1%. This method is designed to reduce the High Confidence False Negatives and improve the reliability of toxic content detection, although it may require more VLM calls.

SynerTCD: Synergistic Toxic Content Detection via Confidence-Aware Routing between CNN and VLM
Feng Zhanga,b, Chaohao Lib,c, Jie Xub,c, Yuantao Jiab,c, Hui Lia, Ning Xia, Shaopeng Zhoub,d, Bin Wanga,b,c

Abstract
Detecting toxic visual content on social media demands both high accuracy and low latency. While Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) offer fast inference, they struggle with semantically complex images containing context, symbols, or embedded text. Conversely, Vision-Language Models (VLMs) provide strong semantic reasoning but incur 10-100× higher computational costs, limiting their scalability. We propose SynerTCD, a two-stage framework that combines the efficiency of CNNs with the accuracy of VLMs through confidence-aware routing. In the first stage, a lightweight CNN processes each image and produces a calibrated confidence score. A routing module forwards only uncertain samples to a VLM for semantic verification in the second stage. Three key innovations enable this design: (i) a calibration-aware CNN training procedure with a dynamic loss selector and a novel Top-Accuracy Calibration Error (TACE) loss that aligns confidence with correctness; (ii) a TAC score based adaptive threshold finder automatically determines the optimal routing boundary for each content category without manual tuning; and (iii) a structured, heatmap-guided prompt template that improves VLM accuracy by 19.4% over baseline prompts. Evaluated on over 207,000 images across nine toxic categories and twelve baselines, SynerTCD reduces inference latency by 87.3% compared to standalone Qwen3-VL-32B while achieving comparable or superior F1 scores on seven categories. Under equal VLM scale, SynerTCD achieves 81% faster inference with +0.5% higher accuracy. Ablation studies confirm the contribution of each component. SynerTCD offers a practical and effective solution for real-world content moderation.
Keywords: Toxic Content Detection, Vision-Language Models, Prompt Engineering, Confidence-Aware Routing.

1. Introduction
With the rapid evolution of social media platforms, user-generated visual content has experienced explosive growth. On platforms such as Instagram, TikTok, X (formerly Twitter), and Reddit, a volume of images and videos is uploaded, shared, and downloaded every minute. This content includes various forms of "toxic content" ranging from pornography, horror, and drugrelated material to gambling advertisements and political extremism. When such toxic content is not detected and removed promptly, the consequences for society can be far reaching. Violence becomes normalized, radical political narratives gain momentum, and minors are exposed to material unsuitable for their age. Regulatory

frameworks such as the European Union's Digital Services Act now mandate that content hosting providers detect and remove illegal or toxic material within hours of notification [1]. Thus, the development of efficient and accurate automated systems for moderating toxic content has emerged as a critical, practical necessity for all media platforms. Deploying such systems at scale presents two challenges. The first is detection scale: the volume of content requiring inspection is enormous, and the screening must happen in real time. The second is semantic complexity: toxic content takes highly diverse forms, and its meaning is often deeply tied to context. For instance, a photograph of a political rally might be entirely innocuous within one cultural context yet highly inflammatory in another; similarly, images depicting drug paraphernalia can sometimes be virtually indistinguishable from ordinary household items. To successfully navigate these two challenges, it is es-

sential to develop capabilities that can adapt the depth of their analysis to the specific scene and content of each sample.
Over the past decade, deep learning has fundamentally reshaped the field of visual content moderation. Within this domain, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have become the backbone of image classification due to their powerful hierarchical feature extraction capabilities. Foundational architectures such as ResNet [2] and EfficientNet [3] established robust baselines through residual learning and principled compound scaling, while lightweight variants such as MobileNetV3 [4] brought these capabilities to resource constrained environments through depthwise separable convolutions [5]. ConvNeXt [6] further advanced the industrial application of CNN. Concurrently, Researchers have also developed practical remedies for severe class imbalance, including Focal Loss reweighting [7] and targeted data augmentation [8]. As illustrated in Figure 1, CNNs are highly efficient and excel at recognizing explicit visual patterns such as flames or firearms, their inherently local receptive fields limit their ability to capture global context, symbolic meaning, and cultural nuance. These limitations become especially pronounced in tasks that demand deep semantic understanding, such as political content analysis [9] and extremist speech detection [10], where the meaning of an image arises not from any single visual pattern but from the interaction of symbols, text, and situational context.
Vision Language Models have opened a fundamentally different avenue by combining visual perception with textual reasoning [11, 12, 13]. By jointly analyzing image content, embedded text, and situational context, VLMs achieve significantly higher accuracy on semantically ambiguous samples [14] and can generate natural language explanations that support interpretable moderation decisions. Recent models such as Qwen3VL [15] and DeepSeek-VL2 [16] have further advanced fine grained perception and reasoning across multiple steps. However, the powerful semantic analysis ability of VLMs comes at a substantial computational cost, the inference time of VLMs is one to two orders of magnitude higher than that of CNNs, making them unsuitable as the only classifier in high throughput pipelines.
Meanwhile, existing research on rapid response engineering has focused primarily on general-purpose benchmarks, leaving the specific adaptability to the often subtle semantic features characteristic of toxic content unexplored. Confidence calibration methods have been designed around single fixed models with fixed global thresholds, where the cost of missing a toxic

To address these challenges, we propose SynerTCD, a framework for Synergistic Toxic Content Detection via confidence-aware routing between CNN and VLM. Building upon the collaborative paradigm introduced in BTCD [18], SynerTCD advances the framework in three key dimensions: (i) a calibration-aware CNN training procedure with a dynamic loss selector and a confidence-routing calibration loss that explicitly aligns confidence scores with prediction correctness; (ii) a TAC score based adaptive threshold finder that automatically determines category-specific routing boundaries, replacing the fixed global threshold; and (iii) a structured four component prompt template guided by CNN-generated heatmaps, which systematically improves VLM detection accuracy by 19.4 percentage points over category name only prompts. The framework is built on the observation that not every image requires the same depth of analysis: a lightweight CNN can handle the vast majority of samples at minimal cost, while the more expensive reasoning power of a VLM should be reserved for genuinely ambiguous cases.
SynerTCD operates as a two-stage online pipeline. In

2

the first stage, a frozen CNN backbone paired with a lightweight MLP classification head processes each input image, produces a detection label together with a calibrated confidence score pi. A routing module then compares this score against an adaptive threshold . When the CNN is confident in its prediction, the result is accepted directly as the final output. When the CNN is uncertain, the sample is forwarded to the second stage, where a VLM refiner performs fine grained semantic verification and generates a natural language explanation for its inferences.
We validate SynerTCD on a benchmark of ten datasets covering one normal category and nine toxic categories, totaling over 207,000 images, and compare it against twelve baselines spanning lightweight CNNs, heavyweight CNNs, Vision Transformers, and VLMs ranging from 0.5B to 32B parameters. SynerTCD matches or exceeds the F1 scores of 32B parameter models on the vast majority of categories while requiring only about 12% of their inference time. In a challenging ten class scenario, SynerTCD reduces inference latency by 8.8% relative to the standalone VLM while simultaneously improving accuracy by 6 percentage points. Ablation studies confirm that each proposed component contributes meaningfully to these results.
The main contributions of this paper are as follows:
· We propose SynerTCD, a cascaded CNN-VLM framework for toxic content detection that routes samples based on calibrated confidence, achieving 81% lower average latency than 32B VLMs while maintaining state-of-the-art accuracy.
· We propose a calibration-aware CNN training method using a dynamic loss selector and a TopAccuracy Calibration Error (TACE) loss that penalizes overconfident errors and maximizes error recall in low-confidence regions.
· We design an adaptive threshold optimization mechanism using a TAC score that automatically determines per-category routing thresholds, eliminating manual tuning and improving crosscategory robustness.
· We develop a structured, heatmap-guided prompt template for VLMs, incorporating role assignment, task decomposition, and conservative bias. This strategy improves VLM accuracy by 19.4 percentage points and remains robust across temperature settings and multi-class scenarios.

2. Related work
2.1. CNN-based Toxic Content Detection
Deep learning has transformed visual content moderation, with Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) becoming the dominant paradigm owing to their powerful feature extraction capabilities. Foundational architectures such as ResNet [2] and EfficientNet [3] established strong baselines through residual learning and principled compound scaling, respectively. Lightweight variants, notably MobileNetV3 [4], extended these advances to resource-constrained platforms via depthwise separable convolutions [5], while ConvNeXt [6] modernized the convolutional paradigm by incorporating Transformer-inspired design choices such as inverted bottlenecks and large kernels. Beyond architecture design, the field has matured toward multi-task formulations that jointly predict fine-grained toxicity categories through shared feature extractors with taskspecific heads, alongside techniques for addressing severe class imbalance, including data augmentation [8] and focal loss re-weighting [7].
However, CNNs' inherently local receptive fields limit their ability to understand global context, symbolic meaning, and cultural nuances. This is particularly evident in tasks requiring deep semantic understanding, such as political content analysis [9]. Although some work has explored extremist speech detection [10] and sentiment analysis [19], CNNs still struggle to accurately classify symbols and behaviors that depend on complex social contexts.
2.2. VLM-based Toxic Content Detection
Vision-Language Models have opened a new paradigm for toxic content detection. CLIP [11] demonstrated that aligning visual and textual embeddings enables zero-shot classification against toxic content descriptors. Generative VLMs advanced this further: BLIP-2 [12] introduced query-based bootstrapping to bridge image encoders with language models; LLaVA [13] and MiniGPT-4 [20] showed that visual instruction

3

tuning produces natural-language explanations valuable for interpretable moderation [14]. More recent architectures including FastVLM [21], DeepSeek-VL2 [16], and Qwen3 [15] have continued to advance efficient alignment, high-resolution perception, and finegrained semantic understanding. Hierarchical Vision Transformers such as Swin Transformer [22] have been widely adopted as visual encoders within VLM pipelines. Beyond detection, VLMs have also been applied to safety-aware generation control [1, 23, 24].
Despite their semantic understanding capabilities, VLMs' computational cost is typically one to two orders of magnitude higher than that of CNNs, which hinders their large-scale deployment. VLMs excel at handling samples that rely on subtle semantic cues, which are challenging for CNNs [25], but their high cost is unnecessary for samples with clear features. Existing research on efficient VLM adaptation [26], confidence calibration [27, 28], and multimodal content analysis [29] provides a foundation for our work.
2.3. Prompt Engineering for VLMs
How a VLM is prompted has proven as consequential as the model's architecture. Learnable softprompt methods formalized this insight early: CoOp [30] showed that optimizing continuous prompt vectors outperforms hand-crafted templates; CoCoOp [31] extended this by conditioning prompts on individual inputs; and MaPLe [32] introduced multi-modal tuning across both vision and language branches. Visual Prompt Tuning [33] demonstrated that prepending learnable tokens to Vision Transformer inputs is an effective adaptation strategy. A parallel, training-free line of research pursued prompt enrichment through LLMgenerated visual descriptions as richer textual anchors [34, 35, 36] and few-shot adaptation without parameter updates [37]. At the reasoning level, Chain-of-Thought prompting [38] has been adapted to VLMs for multistep safety classification, guiding models through scene description, toxic element identification, and toxicity labeling.
A persistent challenge is prompt sensitivity: minor lexical variations can produce drastically different predictions, which is unacceptable in safety-critical settings [30, 31, 35]. Moreover, most prompt engineering research targets general-purpose benchmarks, and its adaptation to the nuanced semantics of toxic content remains largely underexplored.

2.4. Confidence Calibration and Uncertainty Estimation
Well-calibrated confidence scores are essential for systems that must assess trust in their own predictions, especially in toxic content detection. Temperature scaling [27] remains the most widely used post-hoc technique, though modern architectures exhibit qualitatively different miscalibration patterns, tending toward under confidence on in-distribution data and overconfidence on out-of-distribution inputs [17]. Focal loss offers a complementary training-time approach by implicitly improving calibration through down-weighting easy examples [7]. In the VLM domain, CLIP's similarity scores are poorly calibrated as class probabilities, and generative VLMs' verbalized confidence correlates only weakly with actual accuracy [39]. Post-hoc multimodal calibration has been explored but degrades under temporal and demographic shifts inherent to evolving toxic content.
Selective prediction has gained traction as a principled uncertainty-handling strategy. Galil and El-Yaniv [40] studied selective prediction across ImageNet classifiers, while Tian et al. [41] formalized routing uncertain samples to oracles rather than risking incorrect decisions. A common limitation of existing methods is that they apply calibration to a single monolithic model with a fixed global threshold, which is insufficient for toxic content detection where cost asymmetry between false positives and false negatives is extreme and data distributions shift continuously.
Several established paradigms address the trade-off between accuracy and efficiency through staged or conditional inference. Conventional model cascades often arrange models of increasing capacity in sequence and forward a sample to later stages when the current model is insufficiently confident, typically to trade accuracy against computation. Selective prediction allows a model to abstain on low-confidence samples and evaluates risk over the covered samples, leaving abstained cases unresolved unless an additional deferral mechanism is introduced. Early-exit networks typically attach auxiliary classifiers to intermediate layers of a single backbone and terminate inference once an internal prediction is confident enough, thereby reducing the average computational depth. Oracle-routing methods often assume a perfect second-stage expert, while some human-deferral methods model the human as a highly reliable or external decision-maker; in such settings, routed cases are commonly treated as corrected or improved. Unlike standard cascades that often use a global confidence rule to trade accuracy for computation, SynerTCD learns or calibrates confidence estimates for

4

routing and applies category-specific thresholds tailored to the error patterns of toxic categories. Unlike selective prediction, which abstains on low-confidence samples, SynerTCD routes uncertain cases to a vision-language model for refinement and still produces a final decision. Unlike early-exit networks, which reduce computation by exiting early within a single backbone, SynerTCD routes between heterogeneous experts, combining fast visual screening with deeper semantic reasoning. Finally, unlike oracle-routing methods, or deferral settings that treat the second-stage expert as near-perfect, the vision-language model in SynerTCD can both correct and introduce errors, and the evaluation reflects the actual routed outputs. The novelty therefore lies not in the generic use of a two-stage pipeline, but in the taskspecific integration of calibrated confidence estimation, category-specific routing thresholds, and semantic verification for toxic content detection.
In summary, existing approaches face a fundamental tension between the efficiency of CNN classifiers and the semantic reasoning capability of VLMs, while prompt engineering and confidence calibration research have yet to be jointly leveraged within a unified detection framework. The SynerTCD framework proposed in this paper addresses this efficiency-accuracy dilemma by cascading a calibrated CNN and VLM, routing samples adaptively based on prediction confidence so that expensive semantic reasoning is invoked only when genuinely needed, thereby achieving high accuracy while substantially reducing computational overhead.

3.4. VLM Prompt Engineering
Toxic content detection via VLMs is challenging for two reasons: (1) semantic ambiguity toxic indicators can be subtle or context-dependent, making simple binary prompts insufficient; and (2) false-negative bias general-purpose VLMs tend toward conservative predictions and may miss genuinely toxic content. To address these issues, we design a structured prompt template comprising four components (T1-T4), whose individual contributions are validated in the ablation study.
We focus on enhancing VLM's ability to handle complex toxic content detection, ensuring it can accurately follow user instructions. Prompts are fine-tuned to improve VLM's responsiveness to detailed user prompts and complex analysis.

3.4.1. High-Contribution Location Analysis
Based on the trained CNN model, we generate pixellevel heatmaps via LayerCAM [42] for representative high-confidence and low-confidence samples. These heatmaps highlight regions with the highest contribution to the CNN's decision. The salient regions are then converted into natural-language object descriptions, providing spatially grounded cues. Compared with zero-shot or few-shot strategies that rely solely on category names, this heatmap guided approach captures fine-grained spatial information critical for detecting subtle toxic content.

tackling the challenge of accurately describing semantically complex toxic content. This prompt enables the model to more precisely capture and describe toxic content.
T1: Toxic Content Category. We incorporate the category of toxic content as part of the prompt, such as "Accident", "Drug".
T2: Role Assignment. We establish the VLM as a specialized expert in toxic content detection, aligning its output distribution with the detection task. Without an explicit role definition, the VLM's evaluation boundaries may be inconsistent across categories. The prompt begins with a clearly defined role, , "You are an expert in toxic content detection".
T3: Task Transformation. Rather than posing detection as a single binary question, we decompose it into a hierarchical classification and object recognition task that evaluates multiple semantic dimensions. In addition to identifying objects in the image, the VLM is guided to analyze contextual and linguistic cues. Taking political content detection as an illustrative example, the hierarchy comprises four primary categories: (1) Extremist Symbols and Messaging (hate symbols, inflammatory slogans, and discriminatory content), (2) Incitement and Radicalization (calls for violence, polarizing rhetoric, and mobilization tactics), (3) Cultural and Religious Sensitivity (offensive representations, misuse of sacred symbols, and cultural appropriation), and (4) Context and Intent Analysis (gathering purposes, crowd composition, and underlying political motivations). This structured approach guides VLMs to evaluate content across multiple semantic dimensions, ensuring comprehensive coverage of both explicit and implicit toxic indicators.
T4: Conservative Bias. The fourth component embeds a safety-first principle aimed at minimizing false negative. This explicit instruction shifts the VLM's decision boundary toward higher recall, which is desirable because missing genuinely toxic content incurs far greater cost than a false alarm. Moreover, since the CNN classifier primarily relies on visual patterns, the conservative bias encourages the VLM to complement the CNN by leveraging its broader world knowledge for contextual and situational reasoning, thereby reducing the overall false-negative rate.

Evaluation Metrics. Implementation Details: All models are implemented in PyTorch (v1.13) with Python 3.10 on Ubuntu 22.04, running on an Intel® Xeon® Gold 6226R CPU and two NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU with CUDA 12.9 and driver version 575.57.08. We employed linear probing CNN backbones using transfer learning with frozen feature extractors and train only final classification layers. CNNs use SGD (lr=0.001, momentum=0.9), while Swin Transformer uses AdamW (lr=1e-4) with gradient clipping and AMP. CLIP (ViT-B/32) employs linear

probing with AdamW (lr=1e-4). All models are trained on 224×224 images with standard data augmentation, and train = 0.85. CNN training uses a batch size of 128, while all models perform sample-by-sample inference to ensure a consistent and fair measurement of perimage latency. We select MobileNetV3 as our CNN backbone for its computational efficiency and competitive accuracy performance as demonstrated, making it suitable for the first-stage screening task in our cascaded framework. To enable a comparative analysis of the impact of VLM parameter size on accuracy and inference time, experiments in Section 4.2.1 are conducted using both Qwen3-VL-8B and Qwen3-VL-32B as the VLM refiner. For the ablation studies in Section 5, Qwen3VL-8B is employed as the VLM refiner.
For VLM inference, all models are served through the vLLM framework using their native non-quantized precision. Request-level inference caching is disabled so that reported latencies reflect actual per-sample computation rather than reuse of previous results. The sampling temperature is set to 0.02, selected according to the sensitivity analysis in Table 7, with top_p = 0.9. We do not impose an additional task-specific generation cap beyond the maximum generation length, which is set to 4096 tokens. Since inference is performed locally, any generation failure triggers automatic re-inference until a well-formed and definitive result is obtained. The prompt template imposes explicit constraints on the output format, requiring the VLM to conclude with a clear YES or NO decision. The final label is then extracted directly from this constrained output. For confidence calibration, Expected Calibration Error (ECE) is computed using 15 equal-mass bins. The deployment-time routing threshold is selected on the validation set following Eq. 10. Specifically, the trained CNN is applied to the validation split, and its confidence scores, predicted labels, and ground-truth labels are used to compute the TAC Score over candidate thresholds and determine the optimal per-category threshold. Ground-truth labels are used only for validation-time threshold selection and are not used during test-time routing. To ensure a fair comparison, standalone VLM baselines and the VLM stage of SynerTCD use matched prompt templates with the same output constraints, decoding configuration, and latency measurement protocol.

This pattern supports the routing design of SynerTCD, which relies on confidence to separate reliable CNN predictions from samples requiring VLM refinement. Empirically, this enables a favorable accuracy-latency trade-off by preserving the reliability of high-confidence CNN outputs while using the VLM primarily for error-prone cases.

Each category prompt is built through a four-stage interactive optimization with GPT-5, detailed in Appendix F.1. By incorporating visual evidence associated with the toxic signal, the resulting prompts help the VLM more effectively evaluate the low-confidence samples routed by the CNN.

Parallel Binary Voting. The results above reveal that directly training a single ten-class CNN yields poor accuracy, which weakens confidence-aware routing because an unreliable first-stage model provides limited effective filtering.As an alternative, we retain the nine category-specific binary classifiers and run them in parallel, aggregating their outputs into a single tenclass prediction. Figure 9 compares two aggregation schemes. In the naive Top-1 scheme, the final label is selected from the most confident binary classifier output, including the Normal decision when the most confident classifier predicts Normal.This yields only 46.4% accuracy and 12.5% F1. In contrast, the majority-voting scheme lets each binary classifier vote either for its toxic

category or for Normal. Normal is selected when it receives the majority of votes; otherwise, among the toxic-positive classifiers, the final toxic category is chosen using the highest toxic confidence. This raises accuracy to 68.0% and F1 to 44.9%, with a small CNN-side latency of 6.90 ms per sample, which is negligible relative to VLM inference time. It also substantially outperforms the directly trained ten-class CNN evaluated under the same protocol, which achieves 33% accuracy.
Because the voting scheme reuses the confidence scores of the stronger binary classifiers, it preserves a useful confidence signal for routing. Empirically, applying SynerTCD on top of this voting scheme improves overall accuracy to 86.9% and F1 to 82.7%. Crucially, without modifying the VLM refiner, this strategy reduces the VLM Call Rate from 91.2% to 49.7%. These results indicate that decomposing the multiclass task into parallel binary classifications substantially improves the discriminative strength of the CNN stage and makes confidence-aware routing effective again. The parallel binary voting scheme improves CNN-side classification relative to direct ten-class training, while the resulting confidence scores allow SynerTCD to reduce unnecessary VLM calls and recover a favorable accuracy-latency trade-off. Overall, these findings suggest that SynerTCD remains effective and computationally practical in more complex multi-class moderation settings, substantially outperforming the direct ten-class CNN and standalone VLM baselines and supporting its applicability to practical contentmoderation workflows.
6. Conclusion
In this study, we presented SynerTCD, a cascaded framework that synergistically combines the efficiency of CNN classifiers with the semantic reasoning capability of Vision-Language Models for toxic content detection. The framework introduces three key technical contributions: (i) a calibration-aware CNN training phase with a dynamic loss selector and a differentiable TACE loss that aligns confidence scores with actual prediction correctness; (ii) an adaptive threshold finder based on TAC score optimization that automatically determines category-specific routing thresholds; and (iii) a structured four component prompt template (T1-T4) that guides VLMs toward comprehensive, recall-oriented toxic content analysis. Experiments across nine toxic content categories and over 207,000 images demonstrate that SynerTCD (Qwen3-VL-8B) achieves F1 scores comparable to or exceeding 32B VLMs on seven of nine categories while reducing inference time by approximately 87.3%. Ablation studies confirm the contribution of each component and the robustness of the prompt strategy to temperature settings.
7. Limitations and Future Work

The CNN classifier exhibits significant performance degradation in multiclass toxic content detection scenario, causing most samples to be forwarded to the VLM. As a result, SynerTCD achieves only an 8.8% reduction in average inference time compared to the standalone VLM. We regard this multi-class setting as a stress test, where mutually exclusive labels, mixed semantics, and the dominant proportion of Normal samples can weaken the ability of a single ten-class CNN selector to separate reliable from error-prone predictions, thereby increasing the VCR. Replacing the single selector with multiple binary CNN selectors combined through simple voting improves this confidence discrimination and substantially lowers the VCR. This suggests that the current version of SynerTCD is better suited to category-specific binary screening or hierarchical moderation pipelines than to directly replacing a general-purpose multi-class verifier.

Appendix A.2. DataSet Cleaning Protocol
All data were preprocessed through a three-stage cleaning pipeline designed to remove redundancy, enforce quality standards, and improve label reliability.
Duplicate Removal. Redundant images were identified using two fingerprinting methods. MD5 checksums were used to remove exact byte-level duplicates. Near-duplicates were detected using perceptual hashing, where image pairs with pHash Hamming distance  5 were treated as visually identical and grouped for removal. Within each duplicate group, we retained only the image with the largest file size and discarded the rest. Quality Filtering. Images with either spatial dimension below 128 pixels were removed because they provide insufficient visual detail for reliable classification. Each remaining file was then decoded with OpenCV, and files that failed decoding or were unreadable were discarded. Annotation Verification. Initial category labels were verified through a human-in-theloop process using Label Studio. For each image-label pair, three independent annotators reviewed the label blindly, without access to the other annotators' decisions, and flagged cases that did not match the operational category definition. Appendix C. CNN Training Phase Analysis
Appendix C.1. Stability Analysis of CNN Backbones
Figure B.10 reports the stability of the four CNN backbones used in SynerTCD. For each of the nine toxic categories, every backbone is trained with tace for 10 independent runs, and we summarize the average ACC, F1, and inference Time together with the variance across runs. First, the across-run variability of test accuracy and F1 remains very small for every backbone and category, and the error bars are barely visible in most subplots. This indicates that tace produces highly repeatable results and that the reported averages reflect consistent behavior rather than a few favorable runs. Second, the accuracy ranking of the backbones is preserved across all categories, with ResNet50, MobileNetV3-Large, and EfficientNet-B3 achieving comparable ACC and F1 while ConvNeXtTiny trails slightly. The stable accuracy together with the low variance confirms that tace offers backboneagnostic and category-agnostic robustness, allowing SynerTCD to select a backbone based on the accuracy and latency trade-off without concern for training instability.
Appendix C.2. Effect of Backbone Freezing
Figure B.11 compares frozen and unfrozen training settings for the six evaluated visual backbones. Each bar is the average over the nine toxic categories. Across all six backbones, unfreezing the backbone consistently improves both ACC and F1. The gain in ACC ranges from about 0.48 points on EfficientNet-B3 to about 1.44 points on MobileNetV3-Large, and the gain in F1 follows the same trend, for example from 89.16 to 89.57 on EfficientNet-B3 and from 85.18 to 86.34 on CLIPViT-B/32. This confirms that allowing the backbone to adapt to the toxic categories yields more discriminative features than freezing the pretrained weights. Overall, unfreezing the backbone raises the aggregate indicators without a meaningful latency penalty. More importantly, even when every backbone is placed in its stronger unfrozen setting, SynerTCD still surpasses these baselines, which shows that the advantage of SynerTCD comes from its synergistic design rather than from the choice of training configuration alone.

Appendix D. High-Confidence Analysis
Appendix D.1. Qualitative Analysis of HighConfidence CNN Errors
Figure D.12 presents representative cases where the CNN phase is both wrong and highly confident across all nine toxic categories. The first two rows are false positives, in which normal content is reported as toxic, and the last two rows are false negatives, in which toxic content is missed and treated as normal. These examples expose the two failure modes that a confidenceonly threshold cannot catch. A false positive arises when a normal image shares low-level cues with a toxic category, for example a normal sample that contains pills or powder is reported as Drug because its color and texture resemble narcotics. A false negative arises when toxic content is presented in an atypical form or when the abnormal behavior is not visually salient, for example a Drug sample rendered as a comic or one in which the abnormal action is subtle is missed as normal. The same pattern holds for the other categories, where cluttered scenes, ambiguous objects, and stylized imagery drive confident yet incorrect predictions. Because the CNN confidence for the predicted label exceeds the corresponding routing threshold in each case, these errors survive confidence-only routing. Even so, the low selective risk observed under TACE indicates that such highconfidence errors are relatively rare in the CNN stage. Thus, the residual cases shown here represent rare highconfidence failures rather than typical CNN behavior. In this way, the CNN training and calibration strategy keeps the fraction of confidently wrong samples low, while SynerTCD routes mainly low-confidence cases to the VLM rather than invoking it on every sample. This yields an effective trade-off between overall accuracy and inference time.

This point balances the reduction of high-risk CNN acceptances against the need to retain enough correct high-confidence predictions without unnecessarily increasing VLM calls. These distributions explain why the per-category thresholds used in SynerTCD differ across categories and why TACE is essential, since it produces the confidence structure that allows a threshold to route uncertain samples to the VLM while keeping confident CNN predictions in the fast path.

confidence threshold that keeps the VCR within a predefined budget, where VCR is no greater than 15 percent. This method limits the number of samples routed to the VLM while preserving as much performance as possible.
Fixed Error Recall focuses on recalling CNN errors. For each category, it selects the smallest confidence threshold that satisfies the preset CNN error recall requirement, where Error Recall is no less than 80 percent. By using the minimum threshold that reaches the recall target, this method avoids unnecessary VLM calls while ensuring that a sufficient proportion of CNN mistakes can be reviewed.
Validation F1 Maximum selects the confidence threshold for each category by maximizing the routed two-stage F1 score on the validation set. If multiple thresholds achieve the same F1 score, the threshold with the lower VCR is selected. This method directly optimizes the final F1 performance of the routed system.
Cost-based Optimization introduces a cost aware objective to balance accuracy and VLM usage. For each category, the threshold is selected by maximizing ACC - 0.1  VCR. This means that the method rewards higher routed accuracy while penalizing excessive VLM calls.
Uncertainty Estimators treats 1-Con f idence as the uncertainty score of CNN. It assumes that CNN errors tend to have higher uncertainty, and selects the threshold

27

that maximizes T PR_errorFPR_correct. This method chooses the threshold that best separates erroneous CNN predictions from correct ones.
Ensemble Disagreement uses disagreement among multiple CNN models to decide which samples should be reviewed. Specifically, six TACELoss CNN models are used, and samples are sorted from high to low according to prediction disagreement. The number of reviewed samples is then selected to maximize TAC Score.
OOD (Out of Distribution) Detection uses additional out of distribution samples to estimate the threshold. For each binary classification model, 200 samples are randomly sampled from each of the other eight categories. The threshold is then determined by calculating TAC Score on these additional samples.
Class-Specific Safety Thresholds is a safety oriented threshold selection method. For each category, it selects the minimum threshold that simultaneously satisfies two safety constraints. The first constraint is that Error Recall is no less than 95%. The second constraint is that High Confidence False Negatives divided by positives is no greater than 1%. This method is designed to reduce the High Confidence False Negatives and improve the reliability of toxic content detection, although it may require more VLM calls.
Overall, TAC Score provides the most balanced trade-off among accuracy, F1, VCR, and latency. It achieves 97.33% average ACC and 97.21% average F1 with only 18.52% VCR and 350.69 ms average time. Compared with the Fixed Global Threshold, SynerTCD achieves both better performance and lower latency. Compared with Validation F1 Maximum , ClassSpecific Safety Thresholds, and Uncertainty Estimators methods, SynerTCD greatly reduces VLM calls and time consumption while maintaining competitive ACC/F1. Although some methods such as Fixed Error Recall and Uncertainty Estimators obtain slightly higher ACC/F1, they require substantially higher VCR and latency. Therefore, SynerTCD is more suitable for practical deployment because it avoids excessive VLM invocation while preserving high recognition performance.

Appendix F. Prompt Engineering Analysis
Appendix F.1. Prompt Optimization Workflow with LayerCAM
Figure F.21 and Figure F.22 illustrate the offline prompt-generation workflow using the Fire and Horror categories as examples. The detection prompts are produced through an interactive optimization procedure with GPT-5 and are fixed before evaluation. The process has four stages. In the first stage, GPT-5 is asked to define the category in terms of visually observable evidence, producing a structured description that separates core characteristics, derivative characteristics, environmental cues, and edge cases. In the second stage,this description is rewritten using the T2, T3, and T4 prompt strategies defined earlier, adding an expert role, highsensitivity judgment criteria, and a conservative decision rule for ambiguous cases. In the third stage, 100 images whose toxic regions are highlighted by LayerCAM are supplied in 5 batches of 20, which forces GPT-5 to convert the abstract wording into concrete and physically grounded terms drawn from the highlighted evidence, for example open flames, thick smoke, burning objects, and fire damage for the fire category. In the fourth stage, GPT-5 emits the final production prompt together with a fixed output format for the decision. This workflow turns a vague category name into a more precise, evidence-grounded prompt. The LayerCAM highlights ground the refinement process in regions that the CNN considers discriminative, helping the prompt capture concrete visual signals associated with each category. No test images were used during prompt optimization, and the final prompts were frozen before evaluating SynerTCD. The resulting prompts are deployed in the VLM stage of SynerTCD, where they help the VLM handle the low-confidence samples routed by the CNN more consistently.
